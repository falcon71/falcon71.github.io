The KLN 90B in the simulation does not take the aircraft's position from the simulator. It simulates a GPS receiver: satellites in their orbits, the strength of each signal at a tilting antenna, range errors that turn into a position error through the geometry of the satellites, a filter, the search for satellites after power-on, and <Term t="RAIM" />. This page explains that model, its numbers and what each part makes you see on the unit.

<Callout kind="optional">
Nothing on this page is needed to operate the unit. What you need in the cockpit is on [GPS reception and RAIM](/kln90b/operation/gps-receiver/).
</Callout>

## The true and the measured position

The simulation knows two positions: where the aircraft really is, which the simulator says, and where the unit believes it is, which the simulated receiver measures. The measured position is the true one plus the error that the satellites in use cause, smoothed by a filter. Everything the unit shows and sends is based on the measured position: distance and bearing to the waypoints, <Term t="DTK" /> and <Term t="XTK" />, the nearest lists, the airspace alerts, the groundspeed and the track, and the course deviation it sends to your CDI or HSI and to the autopilot. The true position only feeds the model.

With a good sky the two positions lie about 5 m apart. That is less than the smallest step of any distance the unit shows, so you never notice it, but the course deviation and the autopilot work from the measured position, as they would with a real receiver.

The model follows what you do in the simulator. When you pause the simulator, the unit holds its position, groundspeed and track until you continue. When you slew the aircraft or move it to another place, the filter starts over at the new place, so the unit's position follows at once instead of gliding there; the same happens after a gap of more than 30 seconds in the simulator's time. The range errors of the satellites carry on unchanged. Only a new position, after power-on or after the unit has lost its position, starts with new errors.

## The simulated sky

The sky holds 32 satellites. Their orbits come from the ephemeris data that the real GPS satellites broadcast on 1 July 2022, and the simulation moves each satellite along its orbit to the simulator's time. Only the time within the week counts, so the simulated sky repeats every week and the date does not change it. The satellites belong to five generations of GPS satellites, which differ in signal strength (see [Signal strength](#signal-strength)).

The positions of the satellites are recomputed every 5 seconds. That is why the satellites' elevations on STA 1 and the <Term t="EPE" /> on STA 2 change in steps, never in between.

A satellite is in view when it stands high enough above the horizon: 4.5° at sea level. From altitude the horizon lies lower, so a satellite counts as in view down to about 1.4° at FL350. Only the search after power-on uses a fixed limit of 5° for the satellites it expects (see [Finding the satellites](#finding-the-satellites)); once found, a satellite is tracked by this horizon rule. The receiver has eight channels and tracks at most eight satellites at a time. Usually more are in view; it then picks those that give the best geometry, and changes the pick as satellites rise and set. Once the unit navigates, STA 1 lists the satellites of the eight channels.

## Signal strength

Every second the simulation computes for each satellite the signal-to-noise ratio, SNR, in dB-Hz. STA 1 shows it for each satellite in use; a satellite that is not in use shows a `*` and `00`.

<KlnScreen screen="pages/sta/sta-1-nav" rows="2-6" />

The SNR depends first on the satellite's elevation above the antenna. A GPS satellite sends nearly the same power to every place on earth, but the aircraft's antenna receives less toward its own horizon: it falls steeply in the lowest 10° to 20° and is nearly flat above 45°. The simulation uses the curve 50 + 12 × log10(sin elevation), which gives 37 dB-Hz at 5°, 41 at 10°, 46 at 30° and 50 overhead, and never less than 25. The curve and its numbers are the simulation's own choice.

Three more parts make each satellite read a little differently:

- **The generation of the satellite.** The IIR-B satellites read the curve unchanged. The newer satellites of the IIF and IIR-M blocks read 1 dB and 0.5 dB stronger, the IIR-A satellites 1 dB weaker. The newest, GPS III, read 1.5 dB weaker up to 30° elevation and 4 dB weaker from 50° up. A GPS III satellite overhead can therefore read lower on STA 1 than an older one at 30°.
- **A slow fade**, from the reflections off the airframe and the ground. In flight it is about 0.3 dB for a high satellite and 1.2 dB for a low one, and it changes over about 20 seconds. On the ground it is larger, 0.5 to 2 dB, and changes over about 2 minutes.
- **Jitter**: the receiver's estimate of the SNR is itself noisy, by 0.15 dB at 45 dB-Hz and by up to 1.5 dB for the weakest signals.

So the SNR column of STA 1 drifts by a dB or two within a minute, more for the low satellites than for the high ones, and the weakest also flicker from second to second.

<GpsSignalCurve caption="The signal strength over the elevation above the antenna, before the offset of the satellite's generation, the fade and the jitter. A tracked satellite is lost below 30 dB-Hz and found again only at 35; the dots mark where the curve crosses them, at 1.2° and 3.2°. Above 30° the curve rises slowly to 50 overhead." />

One rule decides when a satellite is lost and found, whatever weakened it: a satellite the receiver tracks is lost when its SNR falls below 30, and a lost or new satellite is found again only at 35 or more. The gap of 5 dB keeps a satellite near the limit from coming and going every second. At sea level a satellite rises into view at 4.5° with about 37 dB-Hz, so the horizon decides. From high altitude the signal decides for a rising satellite, which is found at about 3°; a setting one is kept down to the horizon, about 1.4° at FL350.

### The antenna in a bank

The antenna sits on top of the fuselage, and the elevation that counts is the elevation above the antenna's plane, which tilts with the aircraft. In a bank the satellites abeam the low wing climb by the bank angle, and those abeam the high wing sink by it; satellites ahead and behind change less. Pitch does the same fore and aft.

<GpsAntennaBank caption="In a 30° bank to the right, a satellite 15° above the horizon off the left wing stands 15° below the antenna's plane, gets the lowest signal strength and is lost. Its twin off the right wing stands 45° above the plane." />

A satellite abeam the high wing is lost when its elevation is less than the bank angle plus about 1°: in a 30° bank, every satellite abeam the high wing below about 31°. Ahead and behind the loss is smaller. In a steep turn the receiver therefore loses the low satellites on the outside of the turn. STA 1 shows them with a `*`, and the EPE on STA 2 grows as the geometry gets worse. When the remaining satellites are too few, the unit loses its position, as described in [The GPS states](/kln90b/operation/gps-receiver/#the-gps-states). Once you roll out, the signals return, and the receiver has the satellites in use again within about 20 seconds.

## From ranges to a position

A receiver measures its distance, its range, to each satellite. Four unknowns come out of the ranges: the three coordinates of the position and the error of the receiver's own clock. That is why a position needs four satellites, or three and the [altitude input](/kln90b/operation/gps-receiver/#the-altitude-input).

Each range in the simulation has two errors. The slow one stands for the ionosphere and the error of the broadcast ephemeris data: about 4.9 m, and it changes over about 5 minutes. The fast one is the receiver's noise, about 1 m, new every second. Together they make about 5 m per satellite: a single-frequency receiver of today's GPS. The real KLN 90B saw errors many times larger in its first years, until the deliberate degradation of the signals, selective availability, was switched off in 2000; the simulation models the signals as they are now. Each satellite keeps its own slow error while it is in use; a satellite that leaves the solution and comes back starts with a new one.

### The geometry of the satellites

How much position error the range errors make depends on where the satellites stand. Satellites spread over the sky cross their ranges at wide angles, and the errors largely cancel. Satellites bunched in one part of the sky cross at narrow angles, and a small range error moves the position far. The factor between the two is the horizontal dilution of precision, HDOP: the horizontal position error is about 5 m times the HDOP.

<GpsSkyGeometry caption="Two skies seen from above, the horizon as the outer ring, 30° and 60° elevation as the inner rings. Eight satellites spread over the sky give an HDOP of 0.9 and an EPE of 9 m; six satellites bunched to the east give an HDOP of 8.2 and an EPE of 82 m." />

The EPE on STA 2 is twice that typical error: 2 × HDOP × 5 m. Twice the typical error covers about 95% of the cases, and the simulation's own tests show the true error inside the EPE at least 90% of the time. With a normal sky the HDOP is about 1, the EPE about 10 m, and STA 2 shows `0.00NM` or `0.01NM`. The EPE comes from the geometry alone: it does not know the actual error of the moment.

The HDOP and the EPE change only when the geometry changes: when a satellite joins or leaves, and every 5 seconds as the satellites move.

### The altitude as a measurement

With an altitude input, the receiver adds the altitude to its measurements. It weights that row as if the altitude were good to 30 m, six times the error of a satellite's range. With many satellites the altitude therefore counts for little; with three satellites it carries the position. It lowers the HDOP, most when the satellites alone stand poorly.

The error of the altitude is not drawn at random: it is the real difference between the altitude the unit gets, corrected with its baro setting, and the aircraft's true altitude. A wrong baro setting makes it, and so does the simulator's weather, in which the indicated altitude differs from the true altitude, and an encoding altimeter, which reports in steps of 100 ft. A baro setting 1 inHg wrong, about 900 ft, moves the position by less than 25 m with all satellites, and by several hundred meters with three satellites and the altitude. The EPE does not show this, because it knows only the geometry.

## Groundspeed, track and the filter

The receiver measures the velocity, not only the position: it reads it from the Doppler shift of the signals.

<GpsDoppler caption="A satellite moves along its orbit at about 3.9 km/s, and the crests of its signal crowd together ahead of it and spread out behind it. A receiver the satellite is closing on receives a higher frequency, one it is moving away from a lower one. The receiver knows each satellite's motion from its ephemeris data and takes it out; what is left of the shift is the aircraft's own velocity along the line to that satellite. Because the velocity is measured at every moment, groundspeed and track follow a maneuver without lag." />

The simulation gives the velocity from each satellite an error of only 0.05 m/s. The groundspeed and the track come from that velocity, not from the change of the position, so they are accurate to a fraction of a knot and, in cruise, to a fraction of a degree. Standing still, the measured groundspeed is a fraction of a knot; the unit shows a track only from 2 kt, so that the noise of a standing aircraft shows no track.

A filter combines the measured position with the measured velocity, separately for north and east. It moves the position on with the velocity every second and corrects it toward each new measurement, and it is tuned for accelerations of about 3 m/s², a hard maneuver of a light aircraft. Because it trusts the precise velocity, the filter adds no lag of its own: in a takeoff run from 0 to 120 kt the groundspeed stays within 1 kt of the truth.

What the filter smooths are the steps of the measured position. When a satellite joins or leaves the solution, the set of range errors and the geometry change at once, and the measured position steps by a few meters, sometimes some tens of meters. The filter spreads such a step over about 10 seconds in a good sky and over a minute or more in a poor one, because it trusts the measured position less in the direction in which the geometry is poor.

<GpsFilterStep caption="The unit's answer to a step of 20 m to the east in the measured position, with the two skies of the geometry drawing. In the good sky the position is within 1 m of the new value after about 12 seconds. In the poor sky, whose satellites all stand to the east, it has covered 23% of the step after 10 seconds, 52% after 30 and 77% after a minute." />

The filter does not remove the slow range errors, which change over minutes: it cannot tell them from a real movement. The unit's position therefore wanders slowly by a few meters, as a real receiver's does.

## Finding the satellites

After power-on the receiver chooses how to search from what it remembers: the stored position, which it saves every 60 seconds and at power-off, its clock, the almanac data and the ephemeris data of each satellite. [Acquiring satellites](/kln90b/operation/gps-receiver/#acquiring-satellites) gives the times to first fix that a pilot meets; this section explains where they come from.

<GpsSearchFlow caption="The kinds of search after power-on, with the GPS state STA 1 shows for each and the time from power-on to the first fix. A search that finds four measurements, four satellites or three and the altitude input, goes through transition (TRAN) for one second to a navigation state." />

When the ephemeris data is less than 2 hours old and the stored position within 2 NM of where the aircraft is, the start is hot. The receiver finds each satellite in 6 to 12 seconds and can use it at once, and the unit is NAV ready before the Self Test page appears.

In every other start with current almanac data, the time to find a satellite is drawn once at each power-on, between 2 and 75 seconds, and each satellite takes that time within 3 seconds. The draw is common to all satellites because the warm-up of the receiver's oscillator and the moment in the satellites' 30-second data frame are the same for every channel. With ephemeris data less than 2 hours old but a stored position more than 2 NM away, a satellite is usable as soon as it is found, and the first fix comes 2 to about 75 seconds after power-on. Without current ephemeris data each satellite then needs 30 seconds to send it, and the first fix comes after 30 seconds to 2 minutes.

In acquisition (`ACQ`) the receiver searches only the satellites that its almanac data predicts above 5° at the stored position and at the time of its clock. With a stored position far away, it finds those of them that are also in view where the aircraft really is, and the first fix comes when four are found. When fewer than four of the predicted satellites are in view, the receiver finds those and gets no first fix. When the clock is more than 10 minutes off, the predictions are wrong altogether and it finds none. After 5 minutes in `ACQ` it gives up and searches the whole sky blind, in initialization (`INIT`): one satellite after another on its eight channels, 90 seconds give or take 30 for each satellite it finds, and 4 minutes before it gives up on one that is not in view. The first fix comes within about 12 minutes.

When the almanac data is 6 months old or older, the receiver searches blind for one satellite, which takes about 6½ minutes, give or take one. It then reads the almanac data from that satellite for 12.5 minutes, learns the right time with it, and searches as in `ACQ`. The first fix comes after about 20 minutes.

The receiver renews its almanac data whenever it has tracked satellites for 12.5 minutes, and it keeps the ephemeris data of the satellites it tracks. The setting `FAST` on SET 10 is the same receiver with the same times: it only sets the memory to the truth at power-on, so that every start is a hot one ([Real and fast acquisition](/kln90b/operation/gps-receiver/#real-and-fast-acquisition)).

In flight a lost satellite comes back in 6 to 12 seconds while the aircraft is within 2 NM of where the receiver last had a position, and otherwise in about 40 seconds. After it is found, the receiver takes it into the solution at the next update of the satellites, within 5 seconds.

## RAIM inside the receiver

RAIM checks the position with a measurement more than the position needs. With exactly as many measurements as unknowns, every set of ranges fits some position, and a wrong range moves the position without a trace. With one measurement more, the ranges no longer meet in one point when one of them is wrong, and the misfit shows the error.

<GpsRaimSpare caption="Drawn flat, each line is the set of positions that fit one measurement; the ring is the true position, the dot the computed one. Left, with just enough measurements, a wrong one (the thick green line) moves the position and nothing disagrees. Right, with one more, the wrong one misses the crossing of the others, and the misfit reveals it. In the unit, just enough are four measurements and one more is five." />

### When RAIM is available

Detecting an error is not enough: the receiver must detect it before it moves the position by more than the limit of the flight phase, 2 NM en route, 1 NM in the approach arm mode and 0.3 NM in the approach active mode. For each measurement the receiver works out how much an error in it moves the position for each meter of misfit it causes. The largest of these ratios, times the misfit the test is sure to see (5.3 times the 5 m of a range), is the protection level: the position error that the receiver would catch at least 999 times in 1,000. RAIM is available when the protection level lies within the limit of the flight phase.

<GpsProtectionLevel caption="The protection level of the two skies of the geometry drawing, without the altitude input, against the limits of the flight phases. The spread sky gives 0.02 NM, the bunched sky 0.17 NM: RAIM is available even in the approach active mode. Without its highest satellite the bunched sky gives 0.34 NM, too much for the approach active mode; without its lowest it gives 2.53 NM, too much even en route, though its EPE stays at 82 m. Four satellites give no protection level at all." />

The EPE and RAIM measure different things. The EPE says how far the ordinary range errors move the position; the protection level says how well the satellites check each other. The bunched sky has an EPE nine times that of the spread sky and still has RAIM for an approach, and losing its lowest satellite costs RAIM even en route while the EPE does not change.

When the protection level exceeds the limit, STA 1 shows navigation with position degradation, `DEGRD`; with fewer than five measurements there is no protection level, and STA 1 shows no `DEGRD`. The unit shows the protection level nowhere, only its consequences: `DEGRD`, the messages and whether an approach becomes active. A satellite more or less, or a satellite in a better place, can move it across the limit, which is why RAIM comes and goes in a turn or as a satellite sets. The messages that RAIM is not available wait until the condition has lasted more than 10 seconds, so that a satellite that drops out for a moment posts nothing.

### Detecting an error

The test adds up the squares of the misfits of all ranges and compares the sum with a threshold. The threshold is set so high that the ordinary range errors of a few meters would cross it only once in 15,000 checks. In the simulation the test sees only a satellite that sends wrong data: the ordinary range errors and the altitude never enter it. A wrong baro setting therefore moves the position without a RAIM message, and the unit never raises a false alarm. When the test finds an error, the unit posts `RAIM POSITION ERROR` and does not exclude the satellite: it stays in the solution, and its error stays in the position.

### The prediction on STA 5

STA 5 and the activation of an approach predict RAIM with a second, imagined receiver. The unit places it at the waypoint and the ETA to predict, lets it pick its eight satellites from the almanac data as the real receiver would, and runs the same check for the 0.3 NM limit. The imagined receiver is always at sea level, has the altitude input when the unit has one, and knows nothing of the aircraft's attitude or of satellites lost to a weak signal. The 10 seconds that STA 5 takes for its answer are a time the simulation chooses for the unit, not the time the calculation needs.

## Time

The unit has its own clock, which runs on a battery while the unit is off. While the unit has a position, the satellites set the clock every second. If they move it by more than 10 minutes, the unit posts `SYSTEM TIME UPDATED`. Without a position the clock runs on by itself. GPS time in the simulation is the simulator's time.

{/* kln90b#478: describes the current behavior; the fix makes the clock follow the simulator's time without a position too */}

GPS counts weeks only up to 1024, about 19.6 years, and then starts again at zero. The receiver works with that count and takes the era from the unit's clock. A date wrong by a whole 1024 weeks therefore stays wrong after the first position, as happened to older receivers at the rollovers of 1999 and 2019.

## The simulated failures in the model

The simulated failures of the GPS enter the model at the places this page describes: a faulty satellite at the range of one satellite, a jammer at the signal strength of every satellite, a spoofer at both, and the failures of the receiver at the antenna, at the search for satellites or at the whole receiver. That is why each looks different on STA 1, on STA 2 and in the messages. This section explains the mechanism; what each failure does in the cockpit and what ends it are in [What can fail](/kln90b/operation/failures/#what-can-fail), and how to tell them apart on the unit in [Interference and a faulty satellite](/kln90b/operation/failures/#interference-and-a-faulty-satellite).

### A faulty satellite

When the failure `SATELLITE` becomes active, the highest satellite in the solution becomes the faulty one; while the unit has no position, the highest satellite above 5°. The failure imitates a fault of that satellite's clock, whose error grows evenly over a time drawn between 2 and 5 minutes to the time a signal takes for 500 to 3,000 m, a size drawn as well, and then holds. The receiver measures each range by the time the signal was under way, judged by the satellite's clock, so the range it measures to that satellite grows wrong by the same amount.

The satellite's signal does not change: it keeps its signal strength and its health on STA 1, and nothing in the signal says that its clock is wrong. A real satellite is flagged as unhealthy only a while after such a fault, and RAIM is there for that while. STA 1 therefore looks as on a healthy unit, like the list under [Signal strength](#signal-strength):

<KlnScreen screen="gps/failures/satellite" />

The receiver solves for its position as before, and the wrong range moves the position by the satellite's share in the solution. The model puts the clock's error into the range alone and gives the clock no wrong rate, so the Doppler shift, the groundspeed and the track stay right while the position walks away. How far it walks depends on the geometry. With the highest satellite faulty, each meter of range error moves the position by about 0.1 m in the spread sky of [The geometry of the satellites](#the-geometry-of-the-satellites) and by about 1.1 m in the bunched sky. The EPE on STA 2 does not change, because it comes from the geometry alone.

<GpsFailureRamp caption="The position error a faulty satellite causes, for a clock error of the satellite that grows to the equivalent of 2,000 m of range over 200 seconds, in the two skies of the geometry drawing. In the spread sky the position ends 230 m off, in the bunched sky 2,190 m, while the EPE stays at 9 m and 82 m. RAIM detects the fault within about 5 seconds in both skies, once the range error exceeds 40 m and 32 m, when the position is off by about 6 m and 44 m; the message stays while the error goes on growing." />

RAIM sees the fault because the wrong range no longer fits the others. The misfit grows with the range error until the test of [Detecting an error](#detecting-an-error) crosses its threshold, which in the two skies of the drawing happens at a range error of 40 m and 32 m, a few seconds into the ramp. The unit then posts `RAIM POSITION ERROR` at once, without the 10 seconds that the messages of a missing RAIM wait, and keeps it while the error is detected. It does not exclude the satellite, so the error goes on growing in the position under the message.

The test counts only while RAIM is available for the limit of the flight phase. With four measurements, or in a geometry that shows `DEGRD`, the faulty satellite moves the position unseen, and only the messages that RAIM is not available ([RAIM](/kln90b/operation/gps-receiver/#raim)) tell you that nothing checks it.

The fault stays with its satellite while the satellite stays in the solution. When the satellite has been out of the solution for 2 minutes while the unit has a position, because it set or a bank took it, the highest satellite then in the solution becomes faulty, with a new error and a new ramp. The ramp's progress is kept while the unit is off, so it goes on where it stopped on the next flight.

### Jamming

The failure `JAMMING` places a jammer: a transmitter on a mast 100 ft above the ground that sends noise on the GPS frequency. Its noise adds to the noise every satellite's signal is received in, so the SNR of every satellite falls by the same amount, the rise of the noise floor: 10 × log10(1 + J) dB, where J is the jammer's level over the receiver's own noise. The rule of [Signal strength](#signal-strength) does the rest: a satellite whose SNR falls below 30 is lost. The low satellites, whose signals are weakest, go first, and those overhead, about 13 dB stronger, go last.

The jammer's level falls by 6 dB with each doubling of the distance, and the antenna on top of the fuselage hears a transmitter on the ground poorly: 12 dB less than overhead at the antenna's plane, and 0.5 dB less for each degree below it. Beyond the radio horizon, where the earth stands between the mast and the aircraft, the level drops by a further 2 dB for each NM. The radio horizon is 1.23 × (√100 + √h) NM, for a height h in feet above the ground: about 80 NM at 3,000 ft, 135 NM at 10,000 ft and 242 NM at 35,000 ft. A jammer that is strong at its radio horizon therefore takes satellites as soon as you cross the horizon, and a descent below the horizon brings them back.

<GpsFailureHorizon caption="The curvature of the earth between a transmitter and the aircraft, the heights drawn about 24 times too high. The line of sight from the top of the 100 ft mast grazes the earth 12.3 NM away and reaches 3,000 ft above the ground at 79.7 NM, the radio horizon at that height. At 100 NM an aircraft at 3,000 ft is in the shadow, where the signal loses 2 dB for each NM beyond its horizon; one at 10,000 ft, with its horizon at 135 NM, is in sight." />

<GpsFailureJammer caption="The signal strength of three satellites as an aircraft at 3,000 ft closes on the weakest jammer the simulation draws. Little happens until a few NM before the radio horizon at 80 NM; in those few NM the noise floor rises by 10 dB, and the satellite at 5° is lost at once. The satellite at 30° is lost at 38 NM and the one overhead at 23 NM. The position goes when fewer than four measurements remain, RAIM before it." />

A bank tilts the antenna toward the jammer or away from it. A 20° bank toward it raises its level by about 6 to 8 dB, and one away lowers it, so a turn near a jammer can cost satellites that level flight keeps, in addition to those the bank itself takes ([The antenna in a bank](#the-antenna-in-a-bank)).

On STA 1 the SNRs of all satellites fall together, and the low satellites get their `*` first. Here, 50 NM from the jammer at 3,000 ft, the satellites at 5° and 20° are lost, and those at 31° and 76° read 34 and 32 instead of the high 40s:

<KlnScreen screen="gps/failures/jamming" />

As they go, the geometry gets worse: the EPE on STA 2 grows, RAIM goes first, with `RAIM NOT AVAILABLE` `CROSS CHECK POSITION`, and then the position, with `SATELLITE COVERAGE` and the GPS state `ACQ`, and `INIT` after 5 minutes. At 20 NM from the same jammer every satellite is lost:

<KlnScreen screen="gps/failures/jamming-lost" />

No message names the jammer, and RAIM never posts `RAIM POSITION ERROR`: a jammer takes the signals away but leaves every range right. It also leaves the unit's position and time right, so once its noise is gone the position returns within about 40 seconds, even after `INIT`, as after any loss of the signal in flight, and never needs the long search of [Finding the satellites](#finding-the-satellites).

The jammer's strength is drawn when it is placed: it would take the weakest satellite at 100 to 600 NM, measured in a straight line at 35,000 ft as if the earth were not in the way. On the ground it stands 2 to 15 NM away in any direction, within the radio horizon, so that it acts within seconds. In flight it stands ahead of you, within 30° of the track, where its noise rise is only 1 to 3 dB, so that it takes nothing at first and grows as you fly on. When you have flown beyond its reach at the greatest height you have flown since it was placed, but at least at 10,000 ft above the ground, it stays behind and a new one is placed ahead. The jammer and its place are kept while the unit is off.

### Spoofing

The failure `SPOOFING` places a spoofer, a transmitter like a jammer that sends a fake sky: the signals of the satellites as a receiver standing still at another place, the false position, would receive them at another time. The false position lies 30 to 150 NM from the spoofer in a random direction; when an airport lies within 30 NM of that point, the false position is the airport's reference point at its elevation. The false time differs from the true time by 2 to 12 hours, earlier or later.

The fake signals act twice. Together they raise the noise floor of the real satellites, as a jammer does, and each of them is a signal the receiver can lock on to. Their strength does not grow without end: the fake signals interfere with one another, so each tops out at 60 − 10 × log10(n − 1) dB-Hz, however strong the spoofer is, where n is the number of satellites above 5° in the fake sky: 51.5 dB-Hz with eight, 49.6 with twelve, about as strong as a real satellite overhead. The spoofer's strength is drawn to capture the receiver at 300 to 600 NM in a straight line at 35,000 ft, which lies beyond the radio horizon (see the drawing under [Jamming](#jamming)), so every spoofer acts from just outside its radio horizon inward.

A channel that tracks a real satellite keeps it until the rule of 30 dB-Hz loses it; a fake signal takes a channel that is free or still searching at once. A fake satellite is in use about 70 seconds after it takes its channel, the time to find a signal and to collect its ephemeris data. Flying into a spoofer you therefore see three phases:

- **The real satellites drown.** Their SNRs fall, and the low ones are lost first, as with a jammer.
- **The mixed phase.** Fake satellites take the freed channels, and the receiver solves with real and fake satellites together. They claim two different positions and times, so their ranges do not fit: the position and the groundspeed wander off, away from both the true and the false position, and RAIM, where it is available, posts `RAIM POSITION ERROR`.
- **Capture.** Every channel holds a fake satellite. The position jumps to the false position, the groundspeed falls to 0 and the track disappears, because the fake sky is that of a receiver standing still. The satellites set the unit's clock to the false time, which posts `SYSTEM TIME UPDATED`.

In the mixed phase the fake satellites stand out on STA 1 only by their strength: here `01` at 21° reads 48, while the real satellites, weakened by the spoofer's noise, read 35 to 41.

<KlnScreen screen="gps/failures/spoofing-mixed" />

When you fly straight at the spoofer at a jet's speed, the real satellites can all be gone before the first fake satellite is in use; then there is no mixed phase, and the position jumps. When the false time lies far from the true one, few of the satellites the receiver expects are in the fake sky, and the unit has no position at all until it gives up after 5 minutes and searches the whole sky, as in `INIT`; then it is captured.

A captured receiver sees a world that fits: STA 1 shows a navigation state with strong satellites and STA 2 a small EPE, and RAIM finds nothing, because all ranges claim the same false position. One thing on STA 1 gives the spoofer away: all fake signals reach the aircraft at the same level, so every fake satellite reads the same SNR, give or take the jitter, whatever elevation STA 1 shows for it. A fake satellite at 10° reads 50 dB-Hz, where a real one would read about 41; here `03` at 7° reads 50.

<KlnScreen screen="gps/failures/spoofing-captured" />

The one measurement the spoofer cannot fake is the altitude. With an altitude input that differs from the elevation of the false position, the misfit shows, and the unit posts `RAIM POSITION ERROR` for as long as it is captured.

Flying out, the fake signals fall below 30 dB-Hz and are lost, and the position goes with them. The receiver searches from the position and time it believed, finds none of the satellites it expects, searches the whole sky after 5 minutes and comes back to the true position and time, with a second `SYSTEM TIME UPDATED`. The spoofer stays behind once its fake signals are 5 dB below the level at which a satellite is lost, and a new one is placed ahead, by the rules of the jammer. A jammer close by drowns the fake signals as well as the real ones.

### The receiver and the antenna

A failed antenna (`GPS ANTENNA`), a receiver that does not answer (`GPS RCVR`) and a hardware fault that stops the receiver (`RCVR HW`) let no signal through, real or fake. The solution loses every satellite within a second, STA 1 shows `FAILR` with an empty list, and STA 2 shows dashes. Because the unit knows the cause, it posts neither `SATELLITE COVERAGE` nor the RAIM messages; it posts the receiver's own message instead, `RCVR HARDWARE ERROR:` with a code, or `NO RCVR DATA` when the receiver does not answer.

A lower antenna gain (`ANT GAIN`) takes 6 to 15 dB, drawn when the failure becomes active, from every signal, real and fake, as a noise rise would, but the same everywhere and in every attitude. It posts no message: you see it only through the rule of 30 and 35 dB-Hz. With the smallest loss the receiver keeps nearly every satellite, but finds a lost one again only above about 10°. With the largest it loses every satellite below about 22° and finds a lost one again only high in the sky, so after a power-on it finds hardly any. Other failures of the receiver act on the search after power-on ([Finding the satellites](#finding-the-satellites)). A failed receiver memory (`RCVR MEMORY`) loses the almanac data and the ephemeris data, so the first fix needs the search of about 20 minutes. A failed initialization (`RCVR INIT`) keeps the unit's position, time and altitude from the receiver, which then searches from what it remembers by itself and navigates without the altitude input. A failed EEPROM of the receiver (`RCVR EEPROM`) makes finding each satellite take 2 to 4 times as long, until the first fix. When the unit's internal battery has failed (`BATTERY`), the unit loses its memory at power-on as well: the clock starts again at 1 January 1988, so the receiver takes the wrong era from it ([Time](#time)) and the date after the first fix is two rollovers, about 39 years, too early. [The receiver and the antenna](/kln90b/operation/failures/#the-receiver-and-the-antenna) and [The battery](/kln90b/operation/failures/#the-battery) say what each of these does in the cockpit.

## Where the model simplifies

The model reproduces what a receiver shows, not every piece of physics behind it. The orbits are those of one day in 2022 and repeat weekly. Relativity and the errors of the satellites' clocks are left out, except the error of the faulty satellite's clock that the failure adds. That error enters the range alone, where a real clock that ran away like this would also bias the Doppler shift and with it the groundspeed. Every simulated satellite reports itself healthy, the faulty one included. The earth is a sphere for the question of what is in view, and, for the reach of the transmitters, a sphere with a radius a third larger, as is usual for radio waves. Apart from the simulated failures, only the earth, the tilt of the antenna and the fade weaken a signal: mountains and hangars neither block a satellite nor shield you from a jammer. The sizes of the range errors, the antenna curve, the thresholds of 30 and 35 dB-Hz, the factor of RAIM and the false-alarm rate of its test are numbers the simulation chooses, and so are the transmitters' mast, their loss beyond the horizon, the antenna's gain toward the ground and the sizes the failures draw; the limits of 2, 1 and 0.3 NM are the standard ones for GPS receivers of the unit's class.
